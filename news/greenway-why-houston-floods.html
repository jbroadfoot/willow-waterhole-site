Why Houston Floods

Stand on the berm above Westbury Lake on a dry day and it is hard to see what the fuss is about. The lakes look like lakes. The grass looks like grass. Then it rains four inches in an hour, and the reason for all of it becomes obvious.

Houston floods for reasons that are mostly not about weather. The land here is flat, rising only about a foot per mile, so water has nowhere to run to. The soil is clay, which sheds rain rather than absorbing it. The region grew quickly and paved as it went, replacing prairie that soaked up water with roofs, roads and parking lots that do not. And much of the drainage system was built for a smaller city than the one that exists now.

Add a subtropical climate that regularly delivers more rain in a day than some places see in a season, and the result is what the Harris County Flood Control District describes as the county’s number one natural disaster.

The response, over decades, has been to give the water somewhere to go. That is what a stormwater detention basin is. It is a hole, engineered to a specific depth and volume, that fills during a storm and drains slowly afterward, holding back water that would otherwise arrive downstream all at once.

Timing is most of the point. A bayou can carry a great deal of water if it does not have to carry all of it in the same twenty minutes.

Willow Waterhole is one of the detention basins built as part of Project Brays, a roughly $450 million federally funded effort to reduce flooding along Brays Bayou. The Willow Waterhole complex accounts for about $75 million of that. Its six lakes together hold 608 million gallons.

That volume does real work for the neighborhoods around it. It does not make them flood-proof, and nobody involved claims it does. Hurricane Harvey dropped more than two feet of rain on parts of Houston in the first day alone and overwhelmed nearly every mitigation structure in the region. A storm of that size exceeds what any basin is designed for.

What detention does is reduce flooding in the storms that are not Harvey, which is most of them.

The unusual part is what got built on top. There was no requirement that a detention basin have trails, or a birding center, or a prairie preserve, or a concert lawn. That was a choice, made by people who lived nearby and thought the ground could do two things at once.

Next time you visit
The berm along Highway 90 gives the clearest view of how deep the basins actually are. From the top, the difference between water level and design capacity is easy to read.
