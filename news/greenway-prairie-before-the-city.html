The Prairie Before the City

Before Houston, this was grassland.

The Gulf Coast Prairie once ran along the northern rim of the Gulf of Mexico, roughly 6.5 million acres stretching about 75 miles inland, from southeast Louisiana down past Corpus Christi. It was mostly flat and treeless, dominated by subtropical mid- and tallgrasses, broken up by ribbons of forest along the streams. Willow Waterhole Bayou was one of those streams.

That landscape took an enormous span of time to form, shaped by climate shifts, erosion, sediment deposits, and the slow interaction of rivers and ocean with the land. It supported a dense web of plants and animals adapted to fire, drought and periodic flood.

People lived in it. The Karankawa were among the groups who used this stretch of coast, and their name is on one of the trails at Westbury Lake. After roughly three centuries of conflict following European arrival, and around the time Houston was founded in the mid-1800s, the Karankawa left this area and dispersed to the southwest. For a long time they were described as having disappeared entirely.

That was wrong. The Karankawa Kadla, whose name means “mixed,” number more than 100 today and have regrouped near Corpus Christi Bay, where they are working to protect a site that was once a village and where archaeologists have recovered thousands of artifacts: pottery sherds, arrowheads, tools.

The prairie itself fared worse. As Houston grew, the grassland was plowed, grazed, drained and eventually paved. Nationally, roughly one percent of tallgrass prairie remains. The coastal prairie has fared no better, and what survives is fragmented into pieces small enough to lose track of.

Which is why the 15 acres inside the prairie preserve matter out of proportion to their size. That ground is a remnant, not a re-creation. The soil there still held viable seed from the original ecosystem, waiting for someone to cut back the trees and let the light in.

Standing on the boardwalk, it takes some effort to picture 6.5 million acres of this running to the horizon. But the plants at your feet are the same ones, descended in place.

Next time you visit
The Karankawa Trail runs along Westbury Lake. The prairie preserve and its boardwalk sit at the western end of the Greenway, off Prairie Lake.
